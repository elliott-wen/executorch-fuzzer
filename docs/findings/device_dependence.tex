\vspace{2pt}
\raggedright\footnotesize
$^{\dagger}$ The SM8750 run covered 123 of the 128 corpus shards; its deployed count is the number of
graphs in those shards. Restricting instead to the 23 shards of the smaller, fully-verified SM8750 log
gives 95.8\% executed and 91.1\% matched --- the same conclusion.

$^{\S}$ The emulator targets the SM8450, so it is not an independent device: it and the SM8450 phone
agree on 100 of the 104 operators either one flags (96.2\%) and on 7{,}638 of the emulator's 7{,}699
diverging graphs (99.2\%). Taken together as one platform, they diverge on \textbf{48} operators that
the SM8750 gets right, and on none that it gets wrong. The emulator's value as a \emph{numeric}
predictor is high (86.2\% vs 86.6\% matched) but as an \emph{availability} predictor it is poor
(91.0\% vs 96.6\% executed: 609 crashes vs 154, 4{,}915 refusals vs 2{,}169), and those extra
crashes hide real bugs the phone surfaces.

The two Vulkan blocks isolate the two axes of device dependence. Within the single-operator corpus the
three phones refuse an \emph{identical} 5{,}301 graphs (no device-only refusal), so the op surface is
device-independent and the device effect is purely numeric (92.8--95.5\% matched). The capability axis
appears only across \emph{generations}: on the multi-operator corpus the Adreno 630 executes 2.7\%
against 21.5\% for the same-corpus ASUS baseline, because 26{,}681 graphs are refused for a missing
\verb|VK_KHR_8bit_storage| (20{,}389 of them refused on no other device). The drop from 93.1\% to
21.5\% for the \emph{same} ASUS phone is corpus shape, not a device effect: at 19.4 operators per
graph one refused operator refuses the graph, and $0.933^{19.4} \approx 0.26$.
